Question

Show that the shift, substitution, and Vigenère ciphers are all trivial to break using a known-plaintext attack. How much known plaintext is needed to completely recover the key for each of the ciphers?

Attack model

In a known-plaintext attack, the attacker gets pairs $(m, c)$ with $c = \mathsf{Enc}_k(m)$ but cannot choose $m$. Every cipher below encrypts letter by letter, so each pair of letters $(m_x, c_x)$ is one equation in the unknown key. The notation follows Question 1.

Shift cipher

$c_0 = m_0 + k \bmod 26$, so one pair of letters is enough:

$$k = c_0 - m_0 \bmod 26.$$
Needed: 1 letter of known plaintext.

Mono-alphabetic substitution cipher

Every pair gives one value of the permutation: $c_x = \pi_k(m_x)$, so $\pi_k(m_x) = c_x$ is now known. Once $\pi_k$ is known for $25$ different letters, the last value follows because $\pi_k$ is a bijection: it is the one letter that has not been used yet.

Needed: known plaintext that contains at least $25$ different letters.

The attacker cannot choose the text. Rare letters such as q, x or z may show up only after a lot of text, so the amount of plaintext needed can be much larger than 25 letters. However, even a short pair already reveals the most common letters, and these are enough to decrypt most of any ciphertext.

Vigenère cipher

With key $k = (k_0, \dots, k_{t-1})$ we have $c_x = m_x + k_{x \bmod t}$, so every pair gives one key letter:

$$k_{x \bmod t} = c_x - m_x \bmod 26.$$

Any $t$ consecutive letters cover every residue $x \bmod t$ once and therefore give the whole key.

Needed: $t$ letters of known plaintext if the key length $t$ is known. If $t$ is unknown, compute $c_x - m_x$ for every position; this sequence is the key repeated. About $2t$ letters show the first repetition and therefore $t$.

Summary

CipherKey from one pair of lettersKnown plaintext needed
Shift$k = c - m$1 letter
Substitution$\pi_k(m_x) = c_x$text with 25 different letters
Vigenère$k_{x \bmod t} = c_x - m_x$$t$ letters ($\approx 2t$ if $t$ unknown)

In every case the attacker only has to solve one simple equation per letter, without any search or statistics. That is why these attacks are trivial.
