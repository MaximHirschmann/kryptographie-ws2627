Question

A padded-RSA variant: messages $m$ have length $\ell = \|N\|/2$. To encrypt, build $\hat m = 0^{k} \,\|\, r \,\|\, 0^8 \,\|\, m$, i.e. $m$ in the low-order half, a zero byte above it, then 10 random bytes $r$, then leading zeros up to the length of $N$. Output $c = [\hat m^e \bmod N]$. Give a chosen-ciphertext attack. Why is this easier to attack than PKCS #1 v1.5?

Source: Katz & Lindell, 1st ed., Exercise 10.14 (restated).

RSA is multiplicatively malleable: $c \cdot 2^e \equiv (2 \hat m)^e \pmod N$. Multiplying by $2$ shifts $\hat m$ left by one bit, and because $\hat m$ has many leading zeros, there is no reduction modulo $N$.

Attack in $\mathsf{PubK}^{\mathsf{cca}}$

1.Output $m_0 = 00\cdots0$ and $m_1 = 01\cdots1$. Receive $c$.
2.Ask for the decryption of $c' := [2^e \cdot c \bmod N]$ (a different ciphertext).
3.If the answer is an error, output a random bit. Otherwise output the most significant bit of the returned message.

Why it works

$c'$ encrypts $2\hat m$, i.e. $0^k \,\|\, r \,\|\, 0^8 \,\|\, m_b$ shifted left by one bit, read with the same field boundaries:

  • The top bit of $m_b$ is $0$, so the zero byte stays zero, and the new message part is $m_b$ shifted left. Its most significant bit is the second bit of $m_b$, which is exactly $b$.
  • The top bit of $r$ moves into the leading zeros. Decryption reports an error only if that bit is $1$, which happens with probability $\frac12$.
$$\Pr[\text{win}] = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34,$$

so the scheme is not CCA-secure. $\blacksquare$

Why PKCS #1 v1.5 resists this attack

There $\hat m = \texttt{0x00} \,\|\, \texttt{0x02} \,\|\, r \,\|\, \texttt{0x00} \,\|\, m$. Doubling turns the second byte $\texttt{0x02}$ into $\texttt{0x04}$ (or $\texttt{0x05}$), so $2\hat m$ is never correctly formatted and the decryption query always returns an error. (Bleichenbacher's attack on PKCS #1 v1.5 needs many adaptive queries instead.)
