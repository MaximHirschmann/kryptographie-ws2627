Question

A padded-RSA variant: messages $m$ have length $\ell = \|N\|/2$. To encrypt, build $\hat m = 0^{k} \,\|\, r \,\|\, 0^8 \,\|\, m$, i.e. $m$ in the low-order half, a zero byte above it, then 10 random bytes $r$, then leading zeros up to the length of $N$. Output $c = [\hat m^e \bmod N]$. Give a chosen-ciphertext attack. Why is this easier to attack than PKCS #1 v1.5?

Source: Katz & Lindell, 1st ed., Exercise 10.14 (restated).

The encoding as a number

$$\hat m = r \cdot 2^{\ell + 8} + m, \qquad \hat m \lt 2^{\ell + 88} \ll N \approx 2^{2\ell}.$$

Decryption computes $\hat m = c^d \bmod N$, checks the format (leading zeros, then 80 arbitrary bits, a zero byte), and outputs the low $\ell$ bits; otherwise it outputs $\bot$.

Tool: RSA is multiplicatively malleable

$$c \cdot s^e \equiv (\hat m \cdot s)^e \pmod N,$$

so from $c$ anyone can make a ciphertext of $\hat m \cdot s$ for any $s$ without knowing $\hat m$. Since $\hat m$ is tiny compared to $N$, multiplying by a small $s$ causes no reduction modulo $N$: $\hat m \cdot s$ is just the integer product. The format (zeros on top, message at the bottom) survives almost unchanged.

Attack in $\mathsf{PubK}^{\mathsf{cca}}$

1.Output $m_0 = 0^{\ell}$ and $m_1 = 0^{\ell-1}1$. Receive $c^* = \hat m_b^e \bmod N$.
2.Ask for the decryption of $c' := c^* \cdot 2^e \bmod N \ne c^*$. It is the encryption of $2\hat m_b = r \cdot 2^{\ell+9} + 2m_b$.
3.If the answer is $0^\ell$, output $0$; if it is $0^{\ell-2}10$, output $1$; if it is $\bot$, guess.

Why it works. $2\hat m_b$ is $\hat m_b$ shifted left by one bit:

  • low $\ell$ bits: $2m_b \bmod 2^\ell$, which is $0^\ell$ or $0^{\ell-2}10$ (the top bit of $m_b$ is $0$, so nothing is lost);
  • the zero byte receives the top bit of $m_b$, which is $0$, so it stays zero;
  • the top bit of $r$ moves into the leading-zero area. The format check fails only if that bit is $1$, which happens with probability $\frac12$.
$$\Pr[\text{win}] \ge \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34.$$

The advantage $\frac14$ is far from negligible, so the scheme is not CCA-secure. If decryption does not check the leading zeros, the attack always succeeds. Then the same single query even recovers $m$ itself from any ciphertext (all bits except the top one; one more query, e.g. with $s = 3$, gives the top bit). $\blacksquare$

Why this is easier than PKCS #1 v1.5

PKCS #1 v1.5 encodes $\hat m = \texttt{00}\,\texttt{02} \,\|\, \text{(nonzero random bytes)} \,\|\, \texttt{00} \,\|\, m$: the checked structure is at the top of the number, directly below $\|N\|$. Multiplying by any $s \gt 1$ makes $\hat m \cdot s$ overflow and wrap modulo $N$, which destroys the $\texttt{00 02}$ header. Bleichenbacher's attack therefore needs about a million queries, each a random $s$ that passes the header check only with small probability.

In this variant, the structure that decryption checks sits under a large block of zeros, and the message is at the low end. Small multipliers don't wrap modulo $N$, so the result is almost always still well-formed, and a single query reveals the message.

Result. Multiply the challenge by $2^e$: the ciphertext of $2\hat m$ still decodes (with probability at least $\frac12$) and its message part is $2m$. One decryption query distinguishes or even recovers the message. The large zero prefix leaves room for such manipulations, while PKCS #1 v1.5 puts its fixed bytes at the very top, so the attack is much harder there. $\blacksquare$
