Question

The RSA public exponent $e$ only has to satisfy $\gcd(e, \phi(N)) = 1$. In practice $e = 3$ or $e = 2^{16} + 1$ are popular. Explain why these values are better than a random $e$ of the same length.

Source: Katz & Lindell, 1st ed., Exercise 10.8 (restated). Hint: look at the square-and-multiply algorithm.

Cost of square-and-multiply

To compute $x^e \bmod N$, square-and-multiply goes through the bits of $e$ from the top: for each bit after the first it squares, and for each further $1$-bit it also multiplies by $x$. With $\|e\|$ the bit length and $\mathrm{wt}(e)$ the number of $1$-bits:

$$\#\text{modular multiplications} = (\|e\| - 1) + (\mathrm{wt}(e) - 1).$$

Why it matters

  • Speed: $3$ and $65537$ have only two $1$-bits, which minimizes the multiplications for their length. Compared with a "random" $e$ of the same length, about $\mathrm{wt}(e)/2$ multiplications are saved. Compared with a random full-size $e$, encryption and signature verification become roughly 100 times faster.
  • Security is not hurt: the hardness of RSA rests on the secret $d$ and the factorization of $N$. $e$ is public anyway, and $d = e^{-1} \bmod \phi(N)$ is still a large random-looking number. (Small $d$ would be dangerous, small $e$ is not.)
  • Fixed $e$ for everyone: one can choose $p, q$ with $\gcd(e, p-1) = \gcd(e, q-1) = 1$ and can then hard-code $e$.

Why $65537$ rather than $3$?

With textbook RSA, very small $e$ allows attacks: if $m^3 \lt N$, then $c = m^3$ over the integers and $m = \sqrt[3]{c}$; and the same message sent to three recipients with $e = 3$ can be recovered with the CRT (Question 97). Proper padding prevents both, but $65537$ is a common safety margin that is almost as fast.

Result. Exponentiation cost grows with the bit length and the number of $1$-bits of $e$. $3$ and $2^{16}+1$ are short with only two $1$-bits, so public-key operations need only 2 or 17 multiplications, without making the private key easier to find. $\blacksquare$
