Question

Textbook Rabin signatures work like textbook RSA signatures, but with the Rabin function $x \mapsto x^2 \bmod N$: the signer, who knows the factorization $N = pq$, signs a quadratic residue $m$ by outputting a square root $\sigma$ of $m$ modulo $N$; verification checks $\sigma^2 \equiv m \pmod N$. Show that an adaptive chosen-message attack recovers the private key.

Source: Katz & Lindell, 1st ed., Exercise 12.3 (restated).

Fact: square roots reveal the factorization

Every quadratic residue $m \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$ has exactly four square roots $\pm x, \pm x'$ (two modulo $p$ times two modulo $q$, combined by CRT). If $x^2 \equiv \sigma^2 \pmod N$ but $\sigma \not\equiv \pm x$, then $N \mid (x - \sigma)(x + \sigma)$ but divides neither factor, so

$$\gcd(x - \sigma, N) \in \{p, q\}.$$

Attack

1.Choose $x \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_N^*$ and ask for a signature on $m := x^2 \bmod N$ (a quadratic residue by construction).
2.Receive $\sigma$ with $\sigma^2 \equiv m$. If $\sigma \not\equiv \pm x$: output $p = \gcd(x - \sigma, N)$ and $q = N/p$.
3.Otherwise repeat with a new $x$.

Analysis

The signer only sees $m$, and $m$ has four square roots that are equally likely to be our $x$. However the signer picks its root (deterministically or at random), it is independent of which of the four roots we chose. Hence

$$\Pr[\sigma \in \{x, -x\}] = \tfrac{2}{4} = \tfrac12.$$

So each query factors $N$ with probability $\frac12$, and the expected number of queries is $2$. With $p, q$ the attacker knows the private key and can sign anything. $\blacksquare$

Result. Ask for the signature of $x^2$ for a random $x$. With probability $\frac12$ the returned root is not $\pm x$, and $\gcd(x - \sigma, N)$ factors $N$. Textbook Rabin signatures are totally broken by chosen-message attacks. $\blacksquare$

This is the flip side of Rabin's provable security: computing square roots modulo $N$ is equivalent to factoring. A signer that computes square roots for you is therefore a factoring oracle. Secure variants hash the message first (with randomness), so the attacker cannot choose the number being square-rooted.
