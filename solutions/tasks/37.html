Question

Consider a modified substitution-permutation network where the cipher instead first applies $r$ rounds of key-mixing, then carries out $r$ rounds of substitution, and finally applies $r$ permutations. Analyze the security of this construction.

Answer. The construction is completely insecure. It collapses to a single key-mixing step followed by a public, invertible function, so one known plaintext/ciphertext pair reveals the key.

Collapsing the three phases

  • $r$ key-mixing rounds: $x \mapsto x \oplus k_1 \oplus k_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus k_r$. This is the same as one XOR with $K := k_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus k_r$.
  • $r$ substitution rounds: the S-boxes are fixed and public, and they do not use the key. Their composition $S := S^{(r)} \circ \cdots \circ S^{(1)}$ is one fixed, public, invertible function.
  • $r$ permutation rounds: likewise one fixed public bit permutation $P := P^{(r)} \circ \cdots \circ P^{(1)}$.
$$y = P\big(S(x \oplus K)\big).$$

Attack

From one known pair $(x, y)$:

$$K = x \oplus S^{-1}\big(P^{-1}(y)\big).$$

$S^{-1}$ and $P^{-1}$ are public and efficient. Once $K$ is known, every ciphertext can be decrypted, because $K$ is the only key material that matters. The original round keys $k_i$ do not even have to be found. Without a known pair, the attacker can still compute $S^{-1}(P^{-1}(y)) = x \oplus K$ for each ciphertext. That makes the cipher a one-time pad with a reused pad, which is broken by any known structure of the plaintexts.

Why ordinary SPNs are secure

The security of an SPN comes from alternating key mixing, substitution (confusion, non-linear) and permutation (diffusion) in every round. A key added after a non-linear S-box cannot be moved back to the beginning. Grouping the steps by type removes exactly this interplay: all the key mixing happens before any non-linearity, so all non-linear parts become public and can be undone.
