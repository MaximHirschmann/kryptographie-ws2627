Question

A "stronger" Vigenère cipher replaces the shift ciphers by mono-alphabetic substitutions: the key is a list of $t$ independent random permutations $\pi_1, \dots, \pi_t$ of the alphabet, and the plaintext characters in positions $i, t+i, 2t+i, \dots$ are encrypted with $\pi_i$. Explain how to break this cipher.

Source: Katz & Lindell, 1st ed., Exercise 1.3 (restated).

Idea

The attack on the Vigenère cipher has two stages: find the period $t$, then break each of the $t$ streams separately. Both stages still work. The only difference is that each stream is now a mono-alphabetic substitution instead of a shift, so the second stage needs frequency analysis instead of a 26-way search.

Stage 1: find the period $t$

Index of coincidence. For $\tau = 1, 2, \dots$ take the stream $c_1, c_{1+\tau}, c_{1+2\tau}, \dots$, let $q_0, \dots, q_{25}$ be its letter frequencies, and compute

$$S_\tau = \sum_{i=0}^{25} q_i^2.$$

If $\tau$ is a multiple of $t$, every character of the stream was encrypted with the same permutation $\pi_1$. A permutation only relabels the letters, so the multiset of frequencies is the same as in English and

$$S_\tau = \sum_i q_i^2 \approx \sum_i p_{\pi_1^{-1}(i)}^2 = \sum_i p_i^2 \approx 0.065.$$

If $\tau$ is not a multiple of $t$, the stream mixes several unrelated permutations, the frequencies are flattened and $S_\tau \approx \sum_i (1/26)^2 \approx 0.038$. The smallest $\tau$ with $S_\tau \approx 0.065$ is the period.

Kasiski also still works. A word like "the" that occurs twice at the same position modulo $t$ is encrypted with the same permutations both times, so it gives the same ciphertext trigram. The distances between repeated trigrams are multiples of $t$.

Stage 2: break each stream

For $j = 1, \dots, t$, stream $j$ is $c_j, c_{j+t}, c_{j+2t}, \dots$, a mono-alphabetic substitution of every $t$-th plaintext letter under $\pi_j$.

  1. Single-letter frequencies. In each stream, the most frequent ciphertext letter is probably $\pi_j(\texttt{e})$, the next ones $\pi_j(\texttt{t}), \pi_j(\texttt{a}), \dots$. This gives a first guess for the frequent letters of every $\pi_j$.
  2. Use neighbours across streams. A single stream is not consecutive text, so bigrams cannot be seen inside a stream. But consecutive plaintext letters lie in consecutive streams $j, j+1$. Fill in the guesses and read the partially decrypted text: patterns like th_, q followed by u, or common words fix the remaining letters of all permutations together.

How much ciphertext is needed?

Each stream must be long enough for its letter frequencies to be close to English. With a mono-alphabetic cipher that needs a few hundred letters, and here it is needed per stream. So roughly $t$ times as much ciphertext is needed as for a single substitution cipher. The cipher is harder to break, but not secure.

Result. Find $t$ with the index of coincidence (it is invariant under permutations) or with Kasiski. Then attack each of the $t$ streams by frequency analysis, using bigrams across neighbouring streams to finish the permutations. $\blacksquare$

With a known-plaintext attack it is even easier: every known plaintext letter at position $i$ reveals one value of $\pi_{i \bmod t}$ (compare Question 3).
