Question

Show that the shift, substitution, and Vigenère ciphers are all trivial to break using a chosen-plaintext attack. How much plaintext must be encrypted in order for the adversary to completely recover the key? Compare to the amount of plaintext necessary for a known-plaintext attack.

In a chosen-plaintext attack the attacker chooses $m$ and receives $c = \mathsf{Enc}_k(m)$. Letters are numbers, $\texttt{a} = 0, \dots, \texttt{z} = 25$; for any known pair, $k = c - m$.

Shift cipher

Ask for the encryption of one letter, e.g. $\texttt{a}$: the ciphertext is the key. Needed: 1 letter.

Mono-alphabetic substitution cipher

Ask for the encryption of $\texttt{abc}\cdots\texttt{y}$: the ciphertext lists $\pi(\texttt{a}), \dots, \pi(\texttt{y})$, and $\pi(\texttt{z})$ is the one letter still missing. Needed: 25 letters.

Vigenère cipher

Ask for the encryption of $\texttt{aa}\cdots\texttt{a}$: the ciphertext is the key repeated, $k_0 \cdots k_{t-1} k_0 \cdots$. Needed: $t$ letters if the period $t$ is known; if only an upper bound $t_{\max}$ is known, a plaintext of length $O(t_{\max})$ reveals the period as well.

Comparison with the known-plaintext attack

For the shift and Vigenère ciphers every known letter reveals one key letter, so both attacks need the same amount of text. For the substitution cipher, a known plaintext reveals the key only once it contains 25 different letters, which may take a long text (rare letters such as q, x, z). A chosen plaintext needs exactly 25 letters.
