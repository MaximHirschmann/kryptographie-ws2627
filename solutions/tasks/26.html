Question

Prove that if a private-key cipher $\Pi$ can encrypt messages of arbitrary length and the adversary is not restricted to output messages of equal lengths then $\Pi$ cannot have indistinguishable encryptions in the presence of an eavesdropper.

Hint: The answer is not as trivial as one may think at first glimpse. Note that $\Pi$ does not necessarily have to encrypt a message of length $\ell$ into a ciphertext of equal length $\ell$. Consider a polynomial upper bound $p(\cdot)$ on the length of the ciphertext when $\Pi$ is used to encrypt a single bit and use that as a starting point.

Idea

$\mathsf{Enc}$ runs in polynomial time, so the encryption of a single bit has length at most $p(n)$ for some polynomial $p$. Long messages cannot all have such short ciphertexts, because there are too few short strings and decryption must be able to tell them apart. So the length of the ciphertext tells "one bit" apart from "a long message".

Counting

Let $L := p(n) + 2$. Fix any key $k$. By correctness, two different messages can never produce the same ciphertext under $k$. There are fewer than $2^{p(n)+1}$ strings of length at most $p(n)$. So fewer than $2^{p(n)+1}$ messages $m \in \{0,1\}^L$ can have any short ciphertext, i.e. a ciphertext of length at most $p(n)$, under $k$. That is fewer than half of the $2^L = 2^{p(n)+2}$ messages. Averaged over the key and over a uniformly random message $m_1 \leftarrow \{0,1\}^L$:

$$\Pr_{k,\, m_1}\big[\,|\mathsf{Enc}_k(m_1)| \le p(n)\,\big] \lt \frac{2^{p(n)+1}}{2^{p(n)+2}} = \frac12.$$

Adversary

ask$m_0 := 0$ (one bit);   $m_1 \leftarrow \{0,1\}^{p(n)+2}$ uniformly
guesson $c$: output $0$ if $|c| \le p(n)$, otherwise output $1$

$\mathcal{A}$ is PPT, since $p$ is a fixed polynomial.

Analysis

  • $b = 0$: $|c| \le p(n)$ always, so $\mathcal{A}$ is right with probability $1$.
  • $b = 1$: $|c| \gt p(n)$ with probability greater than $\frac12$ (counting above), so $\mathcal{A}$ is right with probability greater than $\frac12$.
$$\Pr[\mathsf{PrivK}^{\mathsf{eav}}_{\mathcal{A},\Pi}(n) = 1] \gt \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34.$$

This is not $\le \frac12 + \mathsf{negl}(n)$, so $\Pi$ does not have indistinguishable encryptions. $\blacksquare$

Why the hint says "not trivial": one cannot simply assume $|c| = |m|$. A scheme might pad every ciphertext to some length. But it can only pad up to a polynomial length, and the adversary simply picks a message longer than that. This is why the definitions require $|m_0| = |m_1|$: encryption may always reveal the length of the plaintext.
