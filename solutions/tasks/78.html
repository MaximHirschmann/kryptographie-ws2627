Question

Let $N = pq$ for distinct primes $p, q$ and $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(N)}$. The book shows $(x^e)^d \equiv x \pmod N$ for $x \in \mathbb{Z}_N^*$. Show that this holds for all $x \in \mathbb{Z}_N$, including those with $\gcd(x, N) \ne 1$.

Source: Katz & Lindell, 1st ed., Exercise 7.10 (restated). Hint: Chinese remainder theorem.

Setup

$ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(N)}$ and $\phi(N) = (p-1)(q-1)$, so

$$ed = 1 + t(p-1)(q-1) \quad \text{for some integer } t \ge 0.$$

By the Chinese remainder theorem, $a \equiv b \pmod N$ iff $a \equiv b \pmod p$ and $a \equiv b \pmod q$. So it suffices to show $x^{ed} \equiv x$ modulo $p$ and modulo $q$.

Modulo $p$

Case $p \mid x$: then $x \equiv 0$ and $x^{ed} \equiv 0 \pmod p$ (since $ed \ge 1$). Both sides are $0$.

Case $p \nmid x$: by Fermat's little theorem $x^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$, so

$$x^{ed} = x \cdot \left(x^{p-1}\right)^{t(q-1)} \equiv x \cdot 1 = x \pmod p.$$

Modulo $q$

The same argument with the roles of $p$ and $q$ swapped (using $x^{q-1} \equiv 1 \pmod q$ when $q \nmid x$) gives $x^{ed} \equiv x \pmod q$.

Combine

$x^{ed} - x$ is divisible by $p$ and by $q$, and $p \ne q$ are primes, so it is divisible by $pq = N$:

$$(x^e)^d = x^{ed} \equiv x \pmod N \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{Z}_N. \quad \blacksquare$$
Result. RSA decryption is correct for every $x \in \mathbb{Z}_N$. Elements not in $\mathbb{Z}_N^*$ are $0$ modulo one of the primes, where the equation holds trivially. $\blacksquare$

Such $x$ are irrelevant in practice anyway: an $x$ with $\gcd(x, N) \notin \{1, N\}$ reveals a factor of $N$ via $\gcd(x, N)$, and a random $x$ is one with probability $\frac{p + q - 1}{N}$, which is negligible. The argument needs $N$ to be squarefree; it fails for $N = p^2$.
